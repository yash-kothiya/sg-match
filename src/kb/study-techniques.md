## Active recall and spaced repetition
Active recall means testing yourself instead of rereading. Close the book and try to explain a topic from memory, answer practice questions, or write down everything you remember, then check what you missed. This effort of retrieving information is what makes it stick. Spaced repetition adds timing: review material at increasing intervals, for example after one day, three days, a week and then two weeks, instead of cramming everything at once. Flashcards are a simple way to combine both ideas, because each card forces you to recall an answer and you can revisit difficult cards more often. In a study group, you can take turns quizzing each other with questions you have prepared, which is active recall with built-in feedback and keeps the session lively.

## Pomodoro and focus blocks
The Pomodoro technique splits work into focused blocks, traditionally 25 minutes of work followed by a 5 minute break, with a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes after four blocks. During a block you work on one task only and keep your phone out of reach. Short, fixed blocks make it easier to start, because you only commit to 25 minutes, and the breaks protect your attention over a long session. If 25 minutes feels too short for deep work, try 45 minutes of work and a 10 minute break. A group can use the same rhythm: work quietly on individual problems during a block, then use the break to compare answers and ask questions. Track how many blocks a task needs so that your plans become more realistic.

## Past-paper practice and teaching to learn
Practising with past exam papers shows you the style of questions, how marks are awarded and where you lose time. Attempt a paper under real conditions, with a timer and no notes, then mark it carefully and list the topics where you lost marks. Revisit those topics before the next attempt. Teaching to learn means explaining a topic to someone else in simple words. If you cannot explain a step clearly, you have found a gap in your understanding. In a study group, assign each person a topic to teach in five minutes and let the others ask questions. Pair this with past-paper practice: after a timed paper, go through the hardest questions together and compare different ways of reaching the answer.
